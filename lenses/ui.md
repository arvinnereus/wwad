# Lens: UI (web & app surfaces)

For landing pages, app screens, dashboards, marketing emails — anything a user looks at. These are the
tests *unique to a screen*. They operationalize the doctrine; they don't restate it. The eight
principles already apply — this is what to look at when the artifact is pixels.

## The squint test
Squint until detail blurs. Is there one obvious focal point, or does everything compete at the same
volume? Apple screens have an unmistakable hero. If yours doesn't survive the squint, the hierarchy is
broken — pick the one thing that matters and make everything else quieter. (This is how "say no" and
"premium restraint" show up on a screen — name the specific elements flattening the hierarchy, not
just "improve hierarchy.")

## Three-second clarity
A first-time visitor should know, within three seconds, what this is and what to do next. If
understanding the screen needs three paragraphs, the message isn't designed yet. One headline that says
the thing plainly beats a clever one that makes them work for it.

## One primary action
Count the calls to action of equal weight above the fold. Apple gives one obvious next step. Two equal
CTAs is the team failing to choose. Demote everything to one primary action and, at most, one quiet
secondary — and name which ones to demote.

## Cut the chrome
Inventory the UI furniture: nav links, badges, "as seen in" logos, banners, cookie bars, chat bubbles,
newsletter popups, spin-to-win wheels. Most can disappear without the user noticing anything but
relief. List every piece and make it justify its place or go. Interrupting overlays (timed popups,
gamified discounts) are the loudest signal of a team that doesn't trust its product to sell itself —
cut those first.

## Motion the user didn't ask for
Auto-rotating carousels, autoplay, and looping animation move the thing the user is trying to read and
hide most of their content at any instant. Replace a carousel with the single strongest still. Reserve
motion for the one moment that earns it (see doctrine #8), not as default decoration.

## Affordance honesty
Do interactive things look interactive, and static things look static? Buttons that read as text, text
styled as buttons, and fake "click me" decoration all erode the "it just works" feeling. Make the real
actions obvious and stop dressing up the rest. (This one is genuinely UI-only — there's no doctrine
principle for it.)
