# Lens: Go-to-market (pricing, naming, positioning, launch)

For names, prices, plans, positioning, and launch stories. These are the GTM-altitude tests. The
doctrine already covers premium-as-a-moat (#5) and the one demo-able moment (#8) — reference those
rather than restating them; the tests below are what's unique to taking something to market.

## The name
Is it simple, ownable, and memorable — iPhone, AirPods, Vision — or a descriptive mouthful that
explains instead of names? Good names are short, easy to say across a noisy room, and hard to confuse.
If the name is doing the work a tagline should do, it's the wrong name. (Naming has no doctrine
principle — this is GTM-only.)

## Pricing legibility
Can a customer understand the price and what they get in a single breath? Count the plans, the add-ons,
and the asterisks. If a spreadsheet is needed to compare options, the pricing is a maze. Apple's tiers
are few and legible, with an obvious "most people want this" middle. (For *whether* to compete on price
at all, that's doctrine #5 — Apple refuses the race to the bottom; find the value story instead.)

## Tier discipline
Too many SKUs, plans, or editions paralyze the buyer and signal a team that couldn't decide who the
product is for. Question every tier: does it serve a genuinely distinct customer, or is it there to
hedge? Fewer, sharper options convert better and feel more confident.

## One-sentence positioning
In one sentence: who is this for, and why is it better for them than the alternative? If it takes a
paragraph, it isn't positioned — it's described. The sentence should be specific enough to *exclude*
the people it's not for. Trying to be for everyone is how you end up for no one.

## The launch story
Compress the whole product into the single concrete line you'd lead the announcement with — the GTM
expression of doctrine #8's "one moment." "A thousand songs in your pocket," not "5GB of storage." If
there's no such line, don't reach for a clever tagline to fake one; the missing line means the
positioning isn't finished yet (see doctrine #8 — fix the substance, not the slogan).
